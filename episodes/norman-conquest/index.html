1066: A Few Thousand Men Erased a Nation of 2 Million

15 October 2026 · 11 min watch · hand-drawn animated history

▶

The short answer

In 1066 somewhere between five and eight thousand men conquered a country of two million people — and kept it. This is the history of the Norman Conquest of England: not the single afternoon at Hastings everyone remembers, but the twenty-year erasure that followed it, ending with only two powerful English landowners left in the whole country.

The story

The Norman Conquest explained, from the succession crisis of January 1066 to the Domesday Book of 1086. How did William the Conqueror take England, and why did the conquest take twenty years instead of one day? This is ancient and medieval military history at its most consequential: the Battle of Fulford Gate, the Battle of Stamford Bridge where Harald Hardrada and the last great Viking invasion died, the nine-hour Battle of Hastings and its shield wall and feigned retreats, the death of King Harold Godwinson and the arrow-in-the-eye myth, the rebellions at Exeter and York, the Harrying of the North, the motte-and-bailey castles, and the great survey that recorded who now owned England, field by field. Along the way: what the Bayeux Tapestry actually is, what it leaves out, and why interest is renewed by its historic loan to the British Museum in 2026 — its first display in England in roughly 950 years. Archaeology, chronicles and the Domesday record together explain how one civilization replaced another's entire ruling class.
