Was Dracula Real?

18 October 2026 · 10 min watch · hand-drawn animated history

▶

The short answer

Dracula was a real prince who used the name himself, but in Bram Stoker's first notes the vampire was called Count Wampyr. The monster you know was assembled from three true stories that never met: a 15th-century prince at war with the Ottomans, a panic over dead villagers dug up by army surgeons, and one Victorian notebook. So was Dracula real, and who finally welded the prince to the vampire?

The story

Was Dracula real? Vlad III of Wallachia, known as Vlad the Impaler, was the son of Vlad Dracul of the Order of the Dragon. We follow the real prince from the Ottoman sultan's court and the night attack near Targoviste in 1462 to the German pamphlets that made him a monster in print, and the empty slab at Snagov. Then where the West's vampire came from: the Habsburg reports from Serbian villages in 1725 and 1732, the second of which brought the word vampire into English. Then Bram Stoker's notes, the 1820 book he read in Whitby, and how the Impaler was linked to Count Dracula only in the 1950s to 1970s, through Bran Castle ('Dracula's Castle') and the book In Search of Dracula. Medieval history, the Ottoman wars, folklore and the history of a legend, and how the film Nosferatu (1922) gave vampires the sunlight rule.

The vampire the West would know was being born somewhere else, two and a half centuries later, in a pile of army paperwork.

Every time you say the word vampire, you are echoing a newspaper from seventeen thirty-two. That is the year the word reached English, in reprints of Austrian army reports about dead villagers in Serbia. The belief was old Slavic folklore, but Western Europe learned it from paperwork. It started in a Serbian village under Habsburg rule. A peasant died, and nine of his neighbours died after him.

So the village dug him up. The body looked undecayed. In front of an imperial official, they staked it and burned it, and the official's report, printed in a Vienna newspaper, used a local word. Vampir. In another Serbian village, Medveda, a former soldier broke his neck falling from a hay wagon. When more deaths followed, his neighbours dug him up too.
